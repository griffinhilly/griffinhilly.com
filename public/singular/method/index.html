Method

How the ratings are built, how they are tested, and how the data are checked.

The rating: SiPM

SiPM rates a player's effect on his team's scoring margin, in points per 100 possessions. Zero is an average player; offense plus defense equals the net rating.

It has two parts. The first is a lineup rating: a regularised plus-minus over every lineup, with a prior that decays over past seasons and an adjustment for shooting luck. The second is a box-score estimate, adjusted for the strength of the opponents. A final layer combines the two, with weights fitted on how well they predict the next season's lineup results, and adds an age curve that differs by player quality.

Projections

This season. The rating for this season is the season model's projection, updated by his games so far. After each game, a game-level rating (a fixed map from one game's box line to SiPM's offense and defense) updates a running estimate, and the projection moves with it. Until 13 November 2026 we added a second one-year step on top. It aged and shrank players twice, so we removed it. Rookies start at the level rookies play at in their first season, which is lower than where they settle after it. We estimate that level from college stats, draft slot and age. Players with no college or international stats on file get draft slot and age only. From the second season on, the outlook uses the level expected after the first year.

Five-season outlook. The mean rating over 2026-27 to 2030-31, with 10th and 90th percentiles, if he plays. It reflects age, current level and how players like him have moved. Since 16 November 2026 the outlook averages two forecasts, half each: this one, which follows players with his rating, age and minutes, and a second that forecasts each part of his game separately (shooting, passing, rebounding, turnovers, rim protection, minutes) and rebuilds the rating from the parts. Players without a rating last season (rookies, returners) keep the first forecast alone. Tested on 11,729 season forecasts one to four years ahead, made in 2013-14 to 2021-22 and fitted only on earlier seasons, the average's typical error is 1.41 points per 100, against 1.44 for the first forecast alone and 1.43 for the second; its 80% range held 83% of outcomes. For the five-season mean the error is 0.91 (first forecast alone 0.94). It mainly helps young stars (25 or younger, rated +4 or better): the first forecast alone came in 0.4 points per 100 too low for them on average, the average 0.2.

Best season ahead. His expected rating in his best later season (2027-28 to 2030-31), the age he is likely to reach it, and the chance that some later season beats this one. A typical path can be flat while the best season still lies ahead: careers zigzag around the trend. All three are fitted on what happened next to players with the same rating, age and minutes since 2007-08. Since 13 November 2026 the chance of a better season also uses a term for star quality. Without it, the chance was too low for players rated +7 or better. Since 16 November 2026 all three are also averaged, half each, with the part-by-part forecast described above. The averaged chance of a better season ran high for players rated +7 or better: in the test seasons it said 44% on average, and 37% of them did have a better later season. It is now recalibrated against those outcomes; tested on seasons left out of the fit, it says 39%.

Projected stats. Usage and minutes come from a trajectory model that includes a role-change block: who left his team, who arrived, and whether the top option is gone. Shooting and per-75 stats come from a short-term box-score forecast that weights recent games more.

Data checks

Every source is audited for errors before use. The audit has traced 76 root causes: 52 in the providers' data and 24 in our own processing. 58 are fixed, 9 excluded, 9 documented, and 0 open. Full audit.

Providers sometimes change data without notice. After our September pull, NBA.com re-issued its 2024-25 player-tracking data on its newer Hawk-Eye definitions. A routine check caught the jump; the 24 affected files were re-pulled and the results built on them re-run. No headline result flipped: the shot-making rating's correlation with the NBA's Hawk-Eye shot-making moved from 0.71 to 0.70.

Appendix: defense before blocks were recorded

The NBA did not record blocks or steals before 1973-74. So single-season ratings for that era lean on rebounds, fouls and team results. They miss rim protection.

Team results means how his teams did in games with him and without him. Before 1996-97, each season rating adds part of this term to his box-score rating. Single seasons use half of the defensive term, because one season of it is noisy.

A career pools many seasons, so the noise averages out. Career defense uses the full term: box-score defense plus team-results defense, averaged over his career. The table ranks 811 players with 15,000+ minutes, 1953-54 to 2025-26. Higher is better.
