Running The Bulls, Ahead of the Herd

The NBA is driven by stars. How do the Chicago Bulls find the next star and put the best supporting cast around him?

Winning in the NBA is a constant battle to scrap for every edge you can possibly find. This is traditionally thought of as the domain of analytics, which is often seen as being in tension with the eye test. In fact, the two are complementary.

An NBA analytics department must have an impact model that blends lineup ratings and box-score data. Mine is on par with the leading public models.

The name came from my hypothesis that was found to be unproven: that identifying the next singular star could be done by data alone. Data narrows the list, shows whose game is still growing, and finds who fits around him. Singularity is identified by watching the game.

1The model

SiPM (Singular Plus-Minus) rates how many points per 100 possessions a player adds, built from public box-score, play-by-play and lineup data. Its forecasts are scored on seasons it never saw. With ratings frozen on every game day of 2025-26 and every week of the two seasons before, it is level with two leading public impact models: DARKO in every replay, and EPM on every 2025-26 date with an archived copy.

Like other impact models, it overvalues non-creating bigs and undervalues primary creators. Finding the underlying cause of these residuals, and framing the data and the analysis accordingly, is a primary focus of this work.

2Findings

Findings at a glance

  • Trajectories. Most explosive box-score traits are highest at 21 or younger and fade first; shooting and passing improve until 26 to 28, ball security until 25; defensive breakouts come about two years after offensive ones.
  • Usage and efficiency. When a teammate's absence hands a player more of the ball, his true shooting falls about half a point per usage point. Most of that comes from losing the teammate who set him up; the extra shots themselves cost little. Comparing players hides the cost.
  • Fit. Spacers and interior bigs add value next to a star creator. Two star creators together are worth about 0.9 per 100 less than their ratings add up to.
  • Threes versus twos. In the half court, threes and twos are now worth the same once offensive rebounds and the opponent's next trip are counted: NBA teams have found the balance. Shots at the rim still beat a three; midrange twos do not. What I want to track is how that balance shifts, so the Bulls can lead the pack on the next play-style revolution.
  • What data cannot see. On defense, award votes add signal the numbers miss. A model built on public data captures about half of the signal in a player's next three seasons.
  • How it was tested. 591 questions tested against the record, many of them answered no. Most numbers carry a 90% interval.

Basketball minds are constantly asking how to play the game better. Those questions are testable, and testing them shows how well the eye test and analytics go together.

The idea the project is named after did not hold

Singular started from a hunch: the NBA, more than other sports, runs on singular talents, so a player whose style is unlike anyone else's should be likelier to become a star. Tested on future All-NBA players, style uniqueness slightly hurt the forecast, and a machine-learned style profile did worse.

That version of the idea could not be proven with public data alone. But the best players in the league plainly each have a unique game, so the nature of top talent remains a guiding principle. There is something there, and it is what I want to keep pursuing.

4What I'm working on further

An analytics department can give the decision makers in the front office invaluable tools. Here are a few, each marked built or proposed.

  • proposed
    Player trajectory profiles. Each player's forecast for next season and beyond, split into shooting, creation, rim pressure, defense, minutes and value. Staff could set a custom path for one part (say a rookie's shooting improves faster than the model expects) and see what it does to the rest and to his long-term value.
  • built
    Cap and rules engine. The current CBA as code, with the league's announced 2026-27 cap, tax and apron figures and every team's cap position by season. It passes 103 of 113 cap-expert edge cases, from the stretch provision and apron trade matching to sign-and-trade hard caps. The other 10 are listed as known gaps.
  • proposed
    Trade-deadline scenario simulator. On that engine: forecast each team's likely moves and run thousands of league-wide deadline scenarios, updated as moves happen.
  • proposed
    Trade fit simulator. Score any trade by fit with the current core: switchable defenders, and spacers and interior bigs next to the star creator (Figures 9 and 10).
  • proposed
    Organization forecasting app. Anyone in the organization would rate players from a phone: underrated, overrated or fair. Each call would be scored as seasons play out, showing which signals and whose judgments predict best.
  • proposed
    More qualitative signals. Coaches' All-Star votes, Most Improved votes and scouting language, each held to the test the All-Defensive votes passed (Figure 11).
  • proposed
    Free-agent supply forecast. Who will reach free agency and when, from the rookie-extension and restricted-free-agency models already fitted.
  • proposed
    Automated briefings. Pre-game and event briefings for every part of the organization: schedule density, altitude back-to-backs, the referee crew's foul rate, and new data as it arrives.
  • proposed
    Defensive matchup ratings, effort and gravity from tracking. Score each defender against each attacker's own usual rates; measure effort as speed and distance on like-for-like plays; measure gravity directly from where defenders stand. Public data cannot separate these from role.
  • proposed
    A day-one plan for the team's tracking feed. A 15-step plan, already written: week one would check the feed against numbers reproduced from public data; month one would build what public data cannot.

Terms used
Per 100 possessions
points scored or allowed per 100 trips down the floor, so fast and slow teams compare fairly.
Net rating
points scored minus points allowed per 100 possessions.
True shooting
shooting efficiency that counts threes and free throws.
Usage
the share of a team's plays a player finishes (shot, free throws or turnover) while he is on the floor.
RAPM
a player's impact measured from the lineups he played in, with teammates and opponents accounted for.
90% interval
the range the true value plausibly falls in; a bar that crosses zero means "cannot tell".
League spread
one standard deviation across players, so traits on different scales can share a chart.
